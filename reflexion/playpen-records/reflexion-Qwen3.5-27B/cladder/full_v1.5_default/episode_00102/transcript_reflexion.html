In this hypothetical scenario, the causal structure is defined as two independent causes (the man and the candle) both directly affecting a single outcome (the room). This structure represents a "common effect" or "collider" configuration where the room is the collider, but more importantly for the estimation of the man's effect, the man and the candle are not causally related to each other (no arrow between them).

Method 1 calculates the marginal association (total correlation) between the man and the room. Since there is no confounding variable (a common cause of both the man and the room) and no mediator, the marginal correlation directly reflects the causal effect of the man on the room.

Method 2 involves conditioning on the candle. While the candle is not a confounder, it is a cause of the outcome. In a standard linear additive model, conditioning on a non-confounder does not bias the estimate. However, in non-linear models or if there is an interaction between the man and the candle, conditioning on the candle can introduce bias or change the interpretation of the effect (e.g., estimating a conditional average treatment effect rather than the marginal one). More critically, if the relationship is non-linear, conditioning on a cause of the outcome can induce a spurious association between the other cause and the outcome if not handled correctly, or simply restrict the variance unnecessarily.

However, the most fundamental reason Method 1 is "more correct" for understanding the general effect of the man is that Method 2 introduces a stratification that is unnecessary and potentially misleading if the goal is the overall causal effect. By conditioning on the candle, you are estimating the effect of the man *within specific levels of the candle*. Unless there is a specific reason to believe the effect of the man depends on the candle (effect modification), the marginal estimate (Method 1) is the most direct and unbiased estimator of the total causal effect in this simple DAG ($Man \to Room \leftarrow Candle$). Conditioning on a variable that is not a confounder (the candle) is generally unnecessary and can reduce statistical efficiency or, in non-linear settings, introduce bias (a phenomenon related to M-bias or collider bias if the structure were slightly different, though here it's primarily about efficiency and the definition of the target estimand).

Therefore, to understand the general effect of the man on the room, Method 1 is the correct approach, while Method 2 is an unnecessary stratification that does not improve the causal estimate and may complicate the interpretation.
